Our lab uses computational models to explain human learning, planning and how they are altered in psychopathology. Located in Bar-Ilan’s brain center, we’re investigating how trait anxiety biases planning content, meta-control, and efficiency. To do so we use online tasks, advanced virtual reality, eye-tracking, and MEG.

World model

The brain’s internal model of how things work: causal knowledge about the self and the world, and what is worth pursuing. It is what lets us plan, imagine and learn without having to act first.

Causal knowledge about the self

Transition priors about oneself: what one can do and what one’s actions bring about — how controllable outcomes are, how one copes, how one is seen by others.

Causal knowledge about the world

Transition priors about the environment: expectations about how situations unfold — what follows what, how likely harm is, and how long it lasts.

Intrinsic goals

What matters at the deepest level — safety, connection, competence, understanding. They set the rewards that every task is evaluated against.

Task representations

The specific model built for the situation at hand. Each inherits its structure from the priors and goals above it, so a bias higher up — overstating threat or understating control, as in anxiety — reshapes every task below.

Using world models to explain human variability and psychopathology

Our recent work models meta-control over when and how to plan, and how worriers remove actions correlated with but not causing threat from task representations used to plan. These studies are motivated by our theoretical account of worry as arising from biased threat-avoidant planning. Ongoing work treats anxiety as a property of a person’s world model, which comprises domain-specific beliefs about threat, control and the self. We are in the process of designing personalized tasks to measure how those idiosyncratic beliefs shape task representations used to learn and plan in biased ways.
