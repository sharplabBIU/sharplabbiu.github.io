      <h2 class="research-title">Delayed Planning</h2>
    </div>
    <p>
      Planning (mentally simulating the consequences of our actions using a model of the world) lets us adapt flexibly to new goals, but it is
      computationally expensive. Efficient decision makers therefore need to know not only how to plan, but when planning is worth doing. We study the
      meta-control computations that determine when to initiate planning. Using a multi-step decision task in which some goals can be reached without
      exerting control at early decision points, we found across two preregistered studies that people learn to defer costly planning until it is
      actually needed, and get better at doing so with experience. Computational modelling shows that this ability is best explained by a model-based
      meta-controller that searches a cognitive map of the task to infer where planning can safely be postponed, rather than by learning from
      experienced outcomes (<a href="https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/8ubct_v1">Sharp &amp; Eldar</a>). Ongoing work asks how the meta-control of
      planning changes with healthy aging, and whether conditions such as anxiety involve planning that is initiated too early or too often.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="research-area">
    <div class="research-header">
      <i class="fa-solid fa-clock-rotate-left research-icon" aria-hidden="true"></i>
      <h2 class="research-title">Offline Learning</h2>
    </div>
    <p>
      Humans learn remarkably quickly, often from only a handful of real-world experiences. One reason is <a
      href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.01643">offline learning</a>: using our memories and a model of the world to simulate actions and their
      outcomes, without receiving new states and rewards from the environment, for example in the pauses between decisions or during rest. Offline
      learning helps resolve the seeming paradox of how we learn so much from so little experience, and lets us build plans and update the value of
      actions far beyond what direct experience alone allows. But because it relies on internal models rather than real outcomes, offline learning can
      also introduce systematic biases. We study how offline learning shapes estimates of action value and uncertainty, and when it leads people
      astray. In ongoing preregistered studies, we test how the time between choices, when offline learning can occur, biases which actions people
      come to prefer, and we examine how offline learning biases may contribute to psychopathology.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="research-area">
    <div class="research-header">
      <i class="fa-solid fa-map-location-dot research-icon" aria-hidden="true"></i>
      <h2 class="research-title">Task Misconstrual in Anxiety</h2>
    </div>
    <p>
      To plan, we rely on internal models of the task at hand: which situations we can be in, which actions are available, and where they lead. We
      propose that chronic anxiety involves systematic distortions of these models. Anxious individuals may misconstrue what a task demands, leading
      them to plan excessively, to revisit decisions they have already made, and to generalize poorly from one situation to the next (see <a
      href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1364661324002924">Sharp, <em>Trends in Cognitive Sciences</em></a>, for how task
      construal can bias planning in chronic anxiety). Our recent work shows that anxiety is associated with biases in how people generalize task
      knowledge (<a href="https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/c2jz5_v1">Schiff &amp; Sharp, <em>Topics in Cognitive Science</em></a>), and that worry is
      associated with excessive model-based revaluation, repeatedly re-checking values that have already been computed. We are building computational
      models that specify how anxious world models are distorted, with the goal of explaining how these distortions give rise to symptoms such as
      worry.
    </p>
  </div>
